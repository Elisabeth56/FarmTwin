# Chunk: Weeding during the vegetative stage

Weed control is one of the most impactful management activities during the maize vegetative stage. The first weeding should occur at approximately 2 to 3 weeks after emergence, before weeds establish deep roots and compete for nitrogen and moisture. A second weeding is typically needed at 5 to 6 weeks after planting, coinciding with the mid-vegetative stage. After canopy closure — when the maize leaves shade the ground between rows — weed pressure drops naturally and further weeding is usually unnecessary. Weeds left uncontrolled during the vegetative stage can reduce yield by 30 to 60 percent in smallholder conditions. Manual weeding with a hoe is standard for smallholder operations, though row spacing at planting should be planned to allow easy movement between rows.

# Chunk: Nitrogen top-dressing at vegetative stage

Nitrogen is the nutrient most limiting to maize yield in Nigerian smallholder systems. A split application is standard: a basal application at or shortly after planting, followed by a top-dressing during the vegetative stage. The top-dressing is typically applied 4 to 6 weeks after planting, coinciding with the period of rapid vegetative growth when nitrogen demand is highest. Urea (46% N) is the most common fertilizer used for top-dressing. Application should be done when the soil has some moisture but is not waterlogged, and fertilizer should be placed 5 to 10 cm from the base of the plant rather than in direct contact with the stem to avoid burning. If heavy rainfall is expected within 24 hours of application, timing should be adjusted to avoid loss through leaching or runoff.

# Chunk: Recognizing nutrient deficiency at vegetative stage

Nitrogen deficiency in maize typically shows first as a yellowing of older (lower) leaves, starting from the leaf tip and progressing along the midrib in a characteristic V-shape. This is because nitrogen is mobile in the plant and gets moved from older leaves to support new growth. Phosphorus deficiency, less common if a basal fertilizer was applied, shows as purpling on the underside of leaves, particularly during cool early growth. Potassium deficiency shows as browning along leaf margins of older leaves. Yellowing that starts on the younger (upper) leaves is more likely to indicate a sulfur or iron issue, or root-zone waterlogging. During the vegetative stage, correcting a nitrogen deficiency with a top-dress urea application can still recover much of the potential yield loss if done early.

# Chunk: Water stress during vegetative growth

Maize during the vegetative stage is more tolerant of short water stress than during flowering, but sustained drought still reduces yield potential meaningfully. Visible symptoms include leaf rolling (leaves curl inward to reduce surface area exposed to sun), a bluish-gray tint to the foliage, and slowed vertical growth. Short dry spells of 3 to 5 days generally do not cause lasting damage if the plant is otherwise healthy. Longer dry spells of 10 or more days during the vegetative stage can reduce final yield by 10 to 30 percent, and set the stage for larger losses if water stress persists into flowering. In smallholder Nigerian systems where irrigation is not typically available, the main mitigations are early planting to align with reliable rainfall, choosing appropriately drought-tolerant varieties, and maintaining good soil cover to reduce evaporation.

# Chunk: Waterlogging and excess rainfall response

Sustained waterlogging of maize root zones causes root asphyxiation and can be highly damaging, particularly during the vegetative stage when the root system is still developing. Symptoms include yellowing and wilting despite obvious water availability, stunted growth, and in severe cases plant death. Fields with poor drainage or low-lying topography are at higher risk. After a heavy rainfall event that leaves standing water on a field, farmers should inspect the field within 24 to 48 hours; if water persists beyond 48 hours, drainage intervention (opening channels to divert water off the field) is warranted where feasible. Waterlogged conditions also substantially increase the risk of fungal disease development in the days that follow, particularly for foliar diseases that benefit from prolonged leaf wetness.
