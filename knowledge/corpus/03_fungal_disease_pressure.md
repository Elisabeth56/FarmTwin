# Chunk: Humidity and rainfall as drivers of fungal disease

Fungal disease pressure on maize is strongly influenced by weather. Most foliar fungal pathogens of maize require prolonged leaf wetness — typically 6 or more continuous hours of surface moisture on the leaf — to establish infection. Sustained relative humidity above 80 percent, particularly overnight, provides the conditions for this. Heavy rainfall events combined with warm temperatures (24-30 °C) create ideal conditions for spore germination and disease establishment. A period of heavy rain followed by warm humid days is a classic high-risk pattern for foliar fungal outbreaks in maize. Farmers who observe this weather sequence — especially during the vegetative or early reproductive stages when the crop canopy is dense and internal humidity is high — should treat it as a signal to inspect the crop for early disease symptoms within 24 to 72 hours.

# Chunk: Northern Corn Leaf Blight (NCLB)

Northern Corn Leaf Blight, caused by Exserohilum turcicum, is one of the most economically important foliar fungal diseases of maize in Sub-Saharan Africa. It thrives in cool to moderate temperatures (18-27 °C) combined with prolonged leaf wetness. Symptoms appear as long, elliptical, gray-green to tan lesions on the leaves, typically 3 to 15 cm long, running parallel to the leaf veins. Lesions first appear on lower leaves and progress upward as the disease develops. Under favorable conditions, the disease can spread rapidly and severely reduce photosynthetic leaf area, particularly damaging if it affects the leaves above the ear during grain filling. Susceptible varieties can lose 15 to 50 percent of yield in a severe outbreak. Vegetative and early reproductive stages are the most important windows for detection and management.

# Chunk: Common rust of maize

Common rust, caused by Puccinia sorghi, is a widespread foliar disease of maize that develops rapidly under moderate temperatures (16-25 °C) combined with high humidity or leaf wetness. Symptoms appear as small (2-5 mm), oval to elongated, cinnamon-brown pustules scattered on both upper and lower leaf surfaces. Pustules rupture the leaf epidermis and release rust-colored spores that can be rubbed off with a finger — a useful field diagnostic. Under favorable conditions, pustule density can become severe enough to visibly discolor the whole leaf, reducing photosynthesis and weakening the plant. Rust pressure is often highest in the middle of the growing season during the vegetative and flowering stages. Most modern maize varieties released by IITA and CIMMYT have moderate to good resistance to common rust, so severe outbreaks are more likely on older or unimproved varieties.

# Chunk: Gray Leaf Spot (GLS)

Gray Leaf Spot, caused by Cercospora zeae-maydis, has become an increasingly important disease of maize across Sub-Saharan Africa, including in Nigeria. It favors warm (25-30 °C) humid conditions with prolonged leaf wetness. Early symptoms are small tan spots on the leaves that expand into narrow, rectangular gray-to-brown lesions running between the leaf veins — the distinctive rectangular shape is a key diagnostic. Lesions can coalesce and destroy large portions of leaf area, particularly on lower leaves first, moving upward. Continuous maize cropping and reduced tillage systems where crop residue is left on the surface increase pressure because the pathogen overwinters on infected residue. Severe outbreaks during grain filling can cut yield by 30 to 60 percent.

# Chunk: How to scout a maize field for disease

Effective scouting for maize disease is done by walking through the field in a systematic pattern rather than only inspecting the edges. A common approach is to walk a diagonal or "W" shape across the field, stopping at 5 to 10 points spread across the area and inspecting 10 to 20 plants at each stop. At each stop, examine both lower and upper leaves; many important foliar diseases begin on lower leaves. Look at both sides of the leaf. Record what you see: the general disease severity, the pattern of distribution across the field, and any specific lesion shapes or spore colors that can help identify the pathogen. Scouting is most valuable when done regularly (weekly at minimum during the vegetative and early reproductive stages) so that any change from baseline is immediately noticeable. High-risk periods — after heavy rainfall or during extended humid weather — warrant more frequent inspection.

# Chunk: Deciding when to act on observed disease

Not every observed lesion warrants intervention. General guidance for smallholder maize is to consider action when disease incidence (percentage of plants showing symptoms) crosses roughly 10 to 20 percent for foliar diseases at the vegetative stage, or when a single plant shows severe lesion coverage on multiple leaves. Early-stage disease with light symptoms and dry weather in the forecast can often be monitored rather than immediately treated. The picture changes when weather conditions favor spread: if lesions are present and the forecast is for continued humidity or rain, action becomes more urgent because pressure will worsen. The decision to intervene weighs the current level of infection, the crop growth stage (early vegetative is more forgiving than late vegetative or flowering), and the near-term weather outlook.
