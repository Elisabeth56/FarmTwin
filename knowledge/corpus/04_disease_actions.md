# Chunk: Preventive cultural practices for fungal disease

Preventive management of fungal disease in smallholder maize centers on cultural practices that reduce the conditions favoring disease. Planting resistant or moderately resistant varieties is the single highest-leverage decision; IITA and NAERLS release variety information that indicates disease resistance profiles. Crop rotation, avoiding continuous maize on the same plot year after year, reduces the buildup of pathogen inoculum in crop residue. Removing or plowing under previous-season residue where possible removes a major overwintering source for pathogens including Gray Leaf Spot and Northern Corn Leaf Blight. Adequate plant spacing (typically 75 cm between rows, 25 cm within rows for smallholder plots) improves air circulation within the canopy and reduces the sustained leaf wetness that pathogens require. Timely weed control reduces humidity within the crop canopy and also removes weed hosts of some pathogens.

# Chunk: Action plan when weather signals high disease risk

When weather patterns signal elevated fungal disease risk — heavy rainfall followed by warm humid days, or sustained overnight humidity above 80 percent for several days — the recommended actions are stepped: First, inspect the crop within 24 to 72 hours, focusing on lower leaves where foliar disease typically first appears. Second, if early symptoms are detected, note the affected area and severity to establish a baseline for tracking whether the situation is worsening. Third, review whether any preventive or cultural interventions still available are worth doing (removing any accessible severely affected plant material, ensuring water is not accumulating in low spots on the field). Fourth, for smallholders with access to fungicide inputs and cost sensitivity, consider whether a preventive fungicide application makes economic sense given the current crop stage, disease pressure observed, and expected market price. In many smallholder Nigerian systems, the primary lever is close monitoring plus cultural interventions rather than fungicide, given cost.

# Chunk: When fungicide use is warranted

Fungicide application on smallholder maize is generally reserved for situations where disease pressure is high, the crop is at a valuable growth stage (typically flowering through early grain fill), and the expected yield loss without treatment exceeds the input cost. For most foliar diseases of maize, protective fungicides applied before or at the earliest visible symptoms are more effective than curative applications after severe damage. Common active ingredients used include chlorothalonil, azoxystrobin, and mancozeb-based products where locally available. Application should follow product labels for rate and re-entry intervals. In smallholder contexts where fungicide is unaffordable or unavailable, the emphasis shifts entirely to preventive cultural practices, resistant varieties, and prompt removal of severely diseased plant material to slow spread.

# Chunk: Recording disease observations for decision-making

Systematic record keeping helps farmers make better decisions across seasons. When a disease observation is made, useful information to record includes: the date, the field or plot affected, the estimated percentage of plants showing symptoms (low: under 10%, moderate: 10-30%, high: over 30%), a description of the symptoms observed (lesion shape, color, distribution on the plant), the current growth stage of the crop, and the recent weather conditions. This record allows a farmer to notice patterns — for example, that a particular field consistently shows earlier or worse disease pressure — and informs decisions about variety choice, rotation, and preventive practices in future seasons. Even simple written or sketched records are more valuable than memory alone.

# Chunk: Variety selection for disease resistance

Modern maize varieties released for Sub-Saharan Africa incorporate resistance to multiple diseases through decades of breeding work at IITA, CIMMYT, and national research systems. Resistance is not absolute — a "resistant" variety can still develop disease under severe pressure — but resistant varieties consistently show lower disease severity and better yield retention than susceptible varieties. When selecting a variety, farmers should consider not only the maturity duration and yield potential but the disease resistance profile relevant to their region. In Northern Nigeria, resistance to Northern Corn Leaf Blight and common rust is broadly valuable; Gray Leaf Spot resistance is increasingly important as the disease spreads. Variety information from NAERLS and IITA typically indicates the resistance profile of released varieties.
