# Chunk: Fall armyworm identification and impact

Fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) has become one of the most economically important pests of maize across Sub-Saharan Africa since its arrival on the continent in 2016. The larvae feed on maize leaves and, at later stages, on the growing point (the "whorl") of the plant. Early signs of infestation include small "windowing" damage where the larvae have scraped the leaf tissue between the veins, small holes in leaves once larvae are large enough to eat through, and characteristic wet-looking frass (droppings) inside the whorl. During the vegetative stage, feeding on the whorl can visibly damage the emerging leaves, which unroll to show ragged, torn edges. Severe infestations can defoliate significant portions of the plant and, if the growing point is destroyed, kill the plant entirely.

# Chunk: Managing fall armyworm in smallholder maize

Fall armyworm management in smallholder systems emphasizes integrated approaches rather than reliance on any single tool. Regular scouting during the vegetative stage — inspecting the whorls of 20 to 50 plants per plot weekly — allows early detection when populations are still manageable. Hand-picking of larvae from the whorl can be effective on small plots when infestation is light. Cultural practices such as intercropping with beans or cowpea, avoiding late planting, and destroying crop residue after harvest all contribute to lower populations. Biological control using natural enemies including parasitoid wasps is being actively promoted across the region. Chemical control, where used, should follow rotation of active ingredients to slow resistance and should not be applied during flowering when pollinators are active. Regional early-warning systems now issue alerts when armyworm outbreaks are developing.

# Chunk: Stem borers on maize

Stem borers, primarily the African stem borer (Busseola fusca) and the spotted stem borer (Chilo partellus) in Nigerian conditions, are established pests of maize. The adult moths lay eggs on the leaves; young larvae initially feed on leaves creating small holes and windowing similar to early armyworm damage, then move to bore into the stem where they feed inside for the remainder of the larval stage. Symptoms of established infestation include small entry holes on the stem, often near a leaf node, with sawdust-like frass visible at the entry point. Severely bored stems weaken and may snap during heavy winds or rainstorms. Damage is most consequential when it occurs during the vegetative and early reproductive stages. Distinguishing armyworm from stem borer early is important because management differs: armyworm feeds in the whorl and is more visible, while stem borer moves inside the stem and is harder to reach once established.

# Chunk: Weekly pest scouting routine

A weekly pest scouting routine during the vegetative stage takes 20 to 40 minutes on a typical smallholder plot and pays for itself many times over. Inspect a representative sample of plants — 5 to 10 stops across the field, 5 to 10 plants at each stop. At each plant, look into the whorl for fall armyworm larvae or their characteristic frass, examine the leaves for feeding damage and windowing patterns, and check the stem base and lower stem for stem borer entry holes. Record what you find: which pest, roughly how many plants affected per stop, and severity. General action thresholds for smallholder maize suggest treatment consideration when about 20 percent of scouted plants show active pest damage, adjusted for growth stage and expected yield value. Below that threshold, continued monitoring and cultural interventions are usually appropriate.
