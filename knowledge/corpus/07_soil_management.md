# Chunk: Sandy loam characteristics and management implications

Sandy loam soils, common across much of Northern Nigeria including Kaduna, are agriculturally productive but require attention to their specific characteristics. They drain well, which is beneficial in reducing waterlogging risk after heavy rainfall, but they also hold less water than heavier soils, meaning dry spells have a more pronounced effect on crops. Nutrient holding capacity is lower than heavier soils, so nitrogen applied in a single large dose is more likely to leach beyond the root zone in heavy rainfall than the same amount applied in split doses. Organic matter content in sandy loams under continuous cultivation is often depleted, which further reduces water and nutrient holding capacity. Practical management centers on split nitrogen applications timed to plant demand, incorporation of organic matter (crop residue, compost, manure where available) between seasons, and maintaining soil cover to reduce evaporation and erosion.

# Chunk: Soil pH and its role in maize productivity

Maize grows best in soils with a pH of 5.8 to 7.0. Below pH 5.5, aluminum toxicity becomes a concern and phosphorus availability drops. Above pH 7.5, micronutrients including zinc and iron become less available. Many soils in Northern Nigeria fall in a broadly favorable range for maize, but areas of intensive continuous cultivation, particularly where fertilizers containing ammonium have been used heavily without organic matter replenishment, can develop acidity over time. Where soil testing indicates acidity below pH 5.5, application of agricultural lime is the standard corrective; typical rates for smallholder application are 500 kg to 2 tonnes per hectare depending on severity, with the effect building over one to two seasons. Farmers without access to soil testing can watch for indirect indicators of acidity such as poor response to fertilizer despite adequate rainfall.

# Chunk: Soil cover and water conservation

Maintaining soil cover between and around maize plants meaningfully reduces evaporation from the soil surface, which extends the effective moisture available to the crop between rainfall events. Options for smallholder systems include leaving crop residue on the surface at planting rather than clearing it entirely, using mulches from grasses or residue where available, and encouraging early canopy closure through appropriate plant spacing. Bare, exposed soil in a Northern Nigerian growing season can lose the top few centimeters of moisture within a day or two of a rainfall event, effectively shortening the useful window between rains. Soil cover also reduces surface crusting after heavy rain, which improves subsequent water infiltration, and reduces soil erosion on sloping fields. These effects are cumulative and are most valuable in years with irregular rainfall.
