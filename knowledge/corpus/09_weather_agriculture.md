# Chunk: Interpreting rainfall events for maize decisions

Not all rainfall events have the same agricultural implication. A light rain of 5-10 mm is often welcome for stand maintenance and reducing plant stress but does little to change soil moisture reserves at depth. A moderate rainfall of 20-40 mm meaningfully replenishes surface and shallow root-zone moisture. A heavy rainfall event of 50 mm or more in 24 hours can be a double-edged sword: it replenishes soil moisture deeply but also increases risk of runoff, leaching of surface-applied fertilizer, waterlogging on poorly drained fields, and elevated fungal disease pressure in the days following. Sustained rainfall over several consecutive days can be more challenging than a single heavy event, particularly for disease pressure. When interpreting a rainfall record, consider not only the total but the intensity (mm per hour where known), the duration, and what conditions immediately preceded and followed it.

# Chunk: Humidity, dew, and leaf wetness

Sustained high humidity — particularly overnight relative humidity above 80 or 85 percent — creates conditions where dew forms on leaves and remains for extended morning hours. Extended leaf wetness (6 or more continuous hours) is the primary requirement for many foliar fungal pathogens to establish infection. Conditions where humidity remains high through the night and morning cloud cover delays evaporation of dew in the morning are particularly favorable for fungal disease. A pattern of humid nights and warm humid days lasting several days should be treated as elevated risk for foliar disease. Farmers can observe humidity indirectly by noticing persistent morning dew, slow drying of laundry or crop surfaces, and by feel; a humid environment is often noticeable without instruments.

# Chunk: Temperature stress on maize

Maize grows best in daytime temperatures of 24-30 °C. Sustained temperatures above 35 °C during the day, particularly during flowering, can cause heat stress that reduces pollen viability and kernel set. During the vegetative stage, moderate heat is generally well-tolerated if soil moisture is adequate; the combination of heat and drought is more damaging than either alone. Cool temperatures below 15 °C slow growth and can prolong the vegetative stage, potentially pushing harvest later than expected. In Northern Nigeria's growing season, temperatures are usually in a favorable range, but late-season heat spikes during flowering can occur and merit attention. There is little a smallholder can do to directly moderate temperature, but variety choice and planting timing can align sensitive stages with historically cooler parts of the season.

# Chunk: Using forecasts under uncertainty

Weather forecasts, particularly for smallholder Sub-Saharan African locations, have inherent uncertainty that grows with the forecast horizon. Next-day and 2-3 day forecasts are generally reliable for whether rain is likely. Week-ahead forecasts are useful for general patterns but specific rainfall totals or timing are much less certain. Agricultural decisions should weight forecasts against how reversible the action is: applying fertilizer just before an unforecast heavy rain is costly to reverse, while delaying a planned inspection by a day is easily reversed. When a forecast suggests high disease-favoring conditions ahead, treating the forecast as a signal to increase vigilance (scouting, monitoring) rather than to make irreversible decisions is generally the right response.
