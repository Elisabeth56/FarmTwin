# Chunk: When to harvest maize

Maize is physiologically mature when the base of the kernels shows a dark "black layer" — a black abscission layer that forms when the plant stops moving photosynthates into the grain. At physiological maturity, kernel moisture is typically around 30 to 35 percent. Harvest for hand-shelling is usually delayed until kernel moisture drops to around 20 percent, at which point the husks have dried and the ears can be broken from the stalk. For storage as shelled grain, further drying to below 14 percent moisture is important to prevent mold and pest damage. Signs that harvest is approaching include browning and drying of husks, drooping of ears, and yellowing/senescence of leaves below the ear. Delaying harvest well beyond maturity increases exposure of the ears to weather, birds, and secondary pests.

# Chunk: Drying and storage basics

Proper drying of maize after harvest is the single most important factor in reducing post-harvest losses. Grain should reach 14 percent moisture or below before extended storage. Traditional practices include sun-drying on tarpaulins or clean flat surfaces, turning the grain periodically to ensure even drying. Elevated drying platforms improve airflow. Once dry, storage in clean sealed containers or in improved storage bags (such as hermetic PICS bags widely promoted for smallholder maize) minimizes losses to insects and moisture reabsorption. Storage in traditional cribs remains common but has higher loss rates from rodents, insects, and weather exposure. Post-harvest losses in unimproved storage can reach 20 to 30 percent of harvested grain, so investment in improved drying and storage often pays back in a single season.
