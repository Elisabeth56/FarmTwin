# Chunk: Cassava planting, weeding and harvest

Cassava should be planted when about two months of soil moisture can be expected. Stem cuttings of about 25 cm are used. Weeding is done at about 4, 8 and 12 weeks after planting. Roots reach their best size at about 9 to 12 months, and some varieties are left 15 to 18 months. Waterlogged soil causes root rot, so cassava belongs on well-drained land. Fresh roots start to deteriorate within 2 to 3 days of harvest.

# Chunk: Cassava pests and the dry season

The common leaf and stem pests of cassava are the cassava mealybug, the cassava green mite, the variegated grasshopper and whiteflies. Mealybug sucks sap from shoot tips, causing bunchy tops, shortened internodes and leaf loss. Green mite feeds under young leaves, leaving yellow speckling, small narrow leaves and bare "candlestick" shoot tips. Both are worse in the dry season and spread on the wind and on infested cuttings. The variegated grasshopper strips leaves and bark, mostly on older plants in the dry season. The whitefly Bemisia tabaci does little direct damage but carries the viruses that cause cassava mosaic disease.

# Chunk: When cassava pest losses are worst and how to limit them

Plants up to about 7 months old are the most vulnerable, because that is when the number of storage roots is set. Planting late, near the end of the wet season, raises losses because the young crop then meets the dry-season peak of mealybug and green mite. The guide's advice is to plant early, at the start of the rains, so plants are vigorous before pests build up. Use cuttings from healthy stems and throw out infested ones. Natural enemies keep the main pests down: a parasitic wasp for mealybug and a predatory mite for green mite, so avoiding needless pesticide sprays protects them. For grasshoppers, dig up egg pods early in the wet season and clear Siam weed, which shelters the young hoppers.

# Chunk: Cassava mosaic disease

Cassava mosaic disease is caused by a virus. Leaves show a mosaic of normal green with yellow or pale patches, become small and distorted, and plants are stunted with poor root yield. The whitefly Bemisia tabaci passes the virus between plants, but the disease spreads mainly through infected stem cuttings. Losses in susceptible varieties range from about 20 to 95 percent. Control rests on resistant improved varieties, taking cuttings only from symptom-free plants, and pulling out and destroying diseased plants.

# Chunk: Cassava bacterial blight, anthracnose and root rots

Cassava bacterial blight shows as angular water-soaked leaf spots that merge into brown blight, leaf wilting and gummy ooze on stems, and can destroy up to the whole crop in susceptible varieties when conditions favour it. The bacteria enter through wounds and spread on infected cuttings, cutlasses and feeding insects such as grasshoppers. The guide advises inspecting fields after the first rains that follow the dry season. Cassava anthracnose causes stem cankers and dieback; its symptoms often start and develop quickly in the wet season, and rain splash and wind carry the spores. Root rots follow waterlogging and late harvest. For all three, use healthy cuttings, remove and burn infected debris, clean tools, and choose well-drained land.
