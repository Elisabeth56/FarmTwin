# Chunk: Yam planting, staking and harvest

Yam is not planted at the very start of the rains; the guidelines wait until rains are well established and steady. In the West African yam belt that puts planting between mid-March and the end of May. Seed tubers go 10 to 15 cm deep on top of a ridge or mound. Each plant gets a long, strong stake, set about 50 cm from the plant when sprouts begin to emerge. During growth the main problems are the yam beetle, yam nematodes and viruses, mostly yam mosaic virus; plants with clear virus symptoms are pulled out and destroyed. Harvest comes after the plants senesce, when all the foliage has dried. After harvest the tubers spend 4 to 6 weeks in a yam barn, then go to a dry, cool store where they are checked weekly for rot, insects and rodents.

# Chunk: Yam anthracnose

Anthracnose is the most devastating fungal disease of yam in West Africa. In severe cases it causes 50 to 90 percent tuber yield loss, and if it strikes early a plant may die without forming tubers. The fungus attacks leaves, stems and tubers at every stage of growth. Water yam (Dioscorea alata) is the most susceptible species, though severe attacks on white yam (D. rotundata) have also been reported in West Africa. Farmers' traditional controls have had limited success, and fungicides have not given lasting control, which is why the review focuses on breeding resistant varieties.

# Chunk: How rain spreads yam anthracnose

The yam anthracnose fungus spreads mainly by rain splash: drops hitting infected leaves throw spores onto nearby plants. Splash spread is local, usually a few tens of centimetres per event, but it is an efficient driver of epidemics because it moves the fungus between neighbouring plants. Spores that land on the soil can be splashed up again by later rain. Bare soil, canopy and plant density all change how far spores travel. How far and how much is spread depends on rain intensity, duration and frequency and on drop size.
